### Foreword

Every good decision begins with the truth about where we stand. Numbers give us that truth. They tell us how many we are, what we earn, what we grow, how our children learn, and how our country is changing. When we know the facts, we choose better, for our families, for our businesses, and for the whole nation.

That is why, as Minister for Finance, I placed Ghana in Numbers at the heart of Government’s plans. In the 2026 Budget, I told Parliament that “in 2026, the GSS will unveil a flagship annual publication, Ghana in Numbers, that tells our national story through data.” This is that publication, and from now on it will be published every year.

Government stands behind this report for a simple reason. Public money and public power must be matched by public facts. When citizens can see the very same figures their leaders use, trust grows and government can be held to account. Openness is the foundation of a confident nation.

Ghana in Numbers is also part of a wider drive to make our statistics stronger and more trustworthy, alongside the rebasing of our national income (GDP) and consumer price figures, and other improvements to the national statistical system. Together, these reforms give Ghana a firmer base of evidence on which to build.

My commitment is plain: the figures in this book will guide the choices we make. Good policy is not built on opinion or guesswork. It is built on evidence. Here, year after year, that evidence is gathered in one trusted place.

This achievement belongs to the Ghana Statistical Service. I thank the Government Statistician, and the management and staff of the Service, for the skill and dedication that turned complex data into a book every Ghanaian can read, and I commend the Board for the leadership and guidance behind this flagship work. Ghana is the better for their effort.

So, I invite every Ghanaian, from student and teacher to trader and farmer to public officer and citizen, to open this book, read it, and use it. Our national story is written in these numbers. Let us read it honestly and together write its next chapter.

HON. DR. CASSIEL ATO BAAH FORSON (MP) MINISTER FOR FINANCE, GHANA

## PDF page 6 — printed page v

Message from the Board Chairman A country cannot govern well what it cannot see clearly. Ghana in Numbers is, for the first time, the whole picture in a single volume.

The essential facts of our national life including how many we are, what we earn and pay, what we produce and trade, how we learn, work, move, and hold power to account, have until now been scattered across censuses, surveys, and administrative records, each sound in itself but rarely read together. This publication brings them into one place and into plain language. It is Ghana, measured.

The Board welcomes it for reasons beyond convenience. It makes official statistics public property rather than the preserve of specialists and gives the country a single authoritative reference that Cabinet and classroom, boardroom and newsroom can reason from, so that debate turns on what the evidence means rather than on whose figures are right. It also reflects the professional independence entrusted to the Service by law: these numbers were produced without fear or favour, and they report what the evidence shows, whether or not it is convenient.

Statistics are never neutral in their consequences. A figure on stunting is a health budget; a figure on youth unemployment is a decision waiting to be taken. This volume will be judged not by copies distributed but by decisions improved. On behalf of the Board, I commend the Government Statistician and the staff of the Service for this great initiative, and I invite every reader to use it and to demand more of it.

DR. ANTHONY YAW BAAH BOARD CHAIRMAN, GHANA STATISTICAL SERVICE

## PDF page 8 — printed page vii

### Preface

Ghana in Numbers asks a simple question: what do the official statistics tell us about Ghana, and how can people use them to make decisions?

The report shows a country of 33.7 million people in 2025. Life expectancy rose from about 40 years in 1960 to 65.1 years in 2021, and nominal GDP per person in 2025 was about nine times its 2013 level. The first Integrated Business Establishment Survey counted 2,644,363 businesses in 2024, three in ten of them working without a building; nine in ten establishments were micro businesses with one to five people, and only 7.7 percent were formal. In public finance, the commitment-basis overall deficit narrowed from 6.9 percent of GDP in 2024 to 1.0 percent in 2025, while the primary balance moved from a deficit of 2.9 percent to a surplus of 2.5 percent. Inflation fell from 54.1 percent in December 2022 to 5.4 percent in December 2025, and lending to businesses and families grew by 13.1 percent after allowing for inflation. However, this lending was 7.4 percent of GDP, and 18.9 percent of bank loans were not being repaid on time. Other chapters show that close to two million people aged 15 to 35 were neither employed nor enrolled in education or training in the third quarter of 2025, more than seven in ten jobs were informal, and about 13 million people faced food insecurity in 2025. The report presents these figures together so readers can see both the changes and the remaining gaps.

We built it for the general reader, not the specialist. Every indicator is given three ways, the number, the trend behind it, and what it means, so that a district officer, a journalist, a student, a business owner, or a minister can reason from the same evidence. You do not need to be a statistician to use this book. That is deliberate.

It also marks how the Service itself is changing. We are shortening the distance between collecting data and publishing it, releasing more data openly through Stats Bank, the GSS website, and the GSS Stats App, and strengthening the wider National Statistical System so that Ghana reasons from one consistent set of numbers. Producing statistics is no longer the finish line. Use is.

This is a first edition, not the last word. Where the evidence is thin, we say so plainly rather than fill the gap with confidence we have not earned. Read it, cite it, argue with it, and tell us what is missing. A statistic changes nothing until someone acts on it, and this volume will be judged not by the copies we distribute but by the decisions it improves.

DR. ALHASSAN IDDRISU GOVERNMENT STATISTICIAN

### Acknowledgements

Ghana in Numbers began with a simple conviction: that official statistics should be legible to everyone with a stake in Ghana's future. Turning that conviction into a published volume drew on the effort and goodwill of many.

We are especially grateful to the Ministry of Finance, and to the Hon. Minister for Finance, for prioritising this work in the 2026 Budget presented to Parliament in November 2025. That decision did more than fund a publication. It affirmed at the highest level of national policy that accessible, reliable statistics are public infrastructure, and it gave the Service the confidence to plan this volume as the first in a continuing series rather than a single undertaking.

The Board of the Ghana Statistical Service shaped the publication from the outset. Its guidance on scope, tone, and audience sharpened what the volume set out to do, and its insistence on both rigour and readability is visible on every page.

Management of the Service provided direction and momentum, holding the work to a demanding standard and an exacting timetable while safeguarding the professional independence on which its credibility rests.

The substance belongs to the technical teams. Statisticians, demographers, economists, and analysts from across the Service compiled, validated, and interpreted the evidence presented here, supported by editorial, design, and communication colleagues who turned technical output into a national story any citizen can read. We thank the reviewers, whose scrutiny strengthened successive drafts, and the institutions and respondents whose cooperation remains the foundation of every official statistic we produce.

To everyone who contributed, named and unnamed, we offer our thanks. The strengths of this publication belong to them; its shortcomings remain ours.

### Executive Summary

Ghana’s population and economy grew, and life expectancy rose over time. At the same time, many young people could not find work. This report brings the figures together so readers can see the changes, the gaps and the choices that the evidence can inform.

Purpose and scope. Ghana in Numbers is the Ghana Statistical Service’s national record book. It brings together the country’s official numbers (on people, health and schooling, the economy, government, the environment, and the Sustainable Development Goals) in one plain-language volume. Big decisions, from the national budget to a single district’s water plan, work best when they rest on good evidence, and these numbers are also how Ghana reports its progress under the SDGs and Agenda 2063.

Population. Ghana’s population reached 33.7 million in 2025, but it is growing more slowly than before: the growth rate fell from 2.7 percent in the 1984–2000 period to 2.1 percent in the 2010–2021 period, so the time it takes to double stretched from 26 to 33 years. More than half the country now lives in towns and cities, and people are living longer: life expectancy rose from 40.3 years in 1960 to 65.1 years in 2021. Young people aged 15 to 35 now number about 12 million, the largest group of young people in the country’s history.

The economy. The economy, in nominal GDP terms, reached about GH¢1.4 trillion in 2025, and income per person rose from GH¢4,710 in 2013 to GH¢42,502 in 2025. Services make up 45.9 percent of what the economy produces, and Ghana sold GH¢148.3 billion more goods to other countries than it bought from them. Not all trade passes a customs desk: a first survey of informal cross-border trade found that goods worth GH¢38.4 billion crossed Ghana’s land borders with Togo, Burkina Faso and Côte d’Ivoire in the year to September 2025, about 6 cedis in every 100 of all the country’s trade and the main way Ghana trades with these three neighbours. Prices are rising much more slowly than before: food prices were climbing at 61.0 percent at their peak in January 2023 and have since calmed down to 4.9 percent by end 2025. Farms keep producing more food, yet the government spent only about 0.2 percent of the economy on agriculture in 2024, less than one cedi in every hundred it spent.

Enterprises. Ghana had 2,644,363 businesses in 2024, according to the first Integrated Business Establishment Survey (IBES I): 1,867,695 establishments working inside fixed structures, 693,748 open-space businesses and 82,920 mobile businesses.

Two limits remained: lending to businesses and families was 7.4 percent of GDP in 2025, down from 10.4 percent in 2022, and 18.9 percent of bank loans were not being repaid on time, compared with 16.0 percent in 2022.

Social progress. Health and schooling have improved a great deal. Mothers dying in childbirth fell from 485 to 301 for every 100,000 births, deaths of children under five halved from 80 to 40 for every 1,000, and active health insurance membership rose from 30.0 percent in 2010 to 66.2 percent in 2025. More than 86 percent of people now have electricity, and about 92 percent of households have safe drinking water.

### 1. Introduction

Ghana makes big development choices quickly, under pressure, and with results that last for decades: where to build the next school and how to pay for it, or which district is falling behind and by how much. Every one of those choices is better when it is based on evidence.

Ghana in Numbers answers a simple problem. The evidence exists, but it has been spread across censuses, surveys, national accounts and official records, each one useful on its own, but rarely read together. This volume brings them into one trusted, plain-language guide to the state of the nation.

It has three aims: to put official statistics into the hands of readers who are not statisticians; to give the country one shared set of numbers, so that debate is about what the evidence means rather than whose figures are right; and to show not just where Ghana stands, but which way it is heading, by presenting change over time.

The book is national in scope. It covers population and society, social development, business and the economy, governance, the environment and climate change, and progress on the Sustainable Development Goals. ### 3.1. A Growing Population

3.1.1. Population Size, (2000–2025) Ghana’s population keeps growing. Every year, hundreds of thousands more people call this country home, and that shapes how many classrooms, clinics, homes and jobs the country needs.

#### Figure 3.1: Population trend 2000 to 2025

Source: GSS, 2010 & 2021 PHC; 2021–2050 Population Projections

Population growth has been steady and unbroken. Ghana adds between half a million and 700,000 people each year, the equivalent of a new city annually. The population rose from 18.9 million in 2000 to 24.7 million in 2010, passed 27 million by 2015, reached 30.8 million in 2021, and is projected at 33.7 million in 2025. Over 25 years Ghana has added almost 15 million people, and in no year did the population fall.

The implication is clear. Ghana is growing at a steady pace, so every plan for schools, clinics, homes, water and jobs must be designed for a larger country each year.

### 3.1.2. Intercensal Growth Rate and Doubling Time

Ghana is still growing, but more slowly with each passing decade. The population grew by 2.7 percent a year between 1984 and 2000, easing to 2.5 percent between 2000 and 2010 and to 2.1 percent between 2010 and 2021. Families are having fewer children, so each year now adds a smaller share of people than before.

Doubling time tells the same story in another way: it is how long the population would take to double if growth never changed. At the rate recorded between 1984 and 2000, Ghana would have doubled in about 26 years; at the 2000 to 2010 rate, 28 years; and at the 2010 to 2021 rate, 33 years. The country therefore continues to grow, but it now takes longer to double, which gives Ghana more time to build the schools, clinics and homes a larger population will need.

### 3.3. Ghana Is Becoming More Urbanised

#### Figure 3.4: Maps showing density population per square kilometre

Source: GSS, 2010 & 2021 PHC

Between the two censuses, every region in Ghana became more crowded. The south stayed the busiest. Greater Accra remained in a class of its own, rising from about 1,236 to 1,681 people per square kilometre. Central followed at 291, then Ashanti at 223 and Volta at 175. The dark blue band along the coast simply got darker.

The north stayed the emptiest. Savannah remained the least dense region in the country, moving only from 13.5 to 18.8 people per square kilometre, while Upper West went from 38.0 to 48.8.

The quieter story is in the growth rates. In absolute terms the south added the most people per square kilometre, but in relative terms the northern regions grew fastest. Northern rose by about 50 percent (58.2 to 87.1) and North East by more than 40 per cent, from a small starting point.

<a id="pdf-page-38"></a>

## PDF page 38 — printed page 17

Ghana has become a country of towns and cities. In 2000 most people still lived in the countryside: 10.63 million in rural areas against 8.28 million in towns and cities. By 2010 the balance had tipped, with 12.55 million urban residents just ahead of 12.11 million rural ones. That was the year Ghana became more urban than rural, a turning point in the country's development.

#### Figure 3.5: Population distribution by type of locality (Urban–rural distribution), 1984– 2025

Source: GSS, 2010 & 2021 PHC; 2021–2050 Population Projections

The shift then accelerated. By 2021, 17.47 million people lived in urban areas and 13.36 million in rural areas. Urban areas continue to grow faster than rural ones. By 2025, they are projected to hold about 19.80 million people, against 13.95 million in rural areas, or close to six in every ten Ghanaians. Growing towns require more housing, water, transport, schools and waste collection, and they require them sooner than before.

#### Figure 3.6: Regional share of population (2010–2025)

Source: GSS, 2010 & 2021 PHC; 2021–2050 Population Projections

Ghana's population is not spread evenly across the country. A few regions hold most of the people, and that pattern has not changed. In 2010 the largest were Ashanti (19.4%), Greater Accra (16.2%) and Eastern (10.7%); the smallest were Savannah (1.9%), North East (1.9%) and Ahafo (2.0%). By 2021 Greater Accra (17.7%) had edged past Ashanti (17.6%) to become the most populous region, Eastern had slipped to 9.5 percent, while the smallest regions changed little.

By 2025, Greater Accra (18.25%) and Ashanti (17.0%) are projected to remain well ahead, followed by Eastern (9.1%). The smallest regions hold steady: Ahafo 1.8 percent, Savannah 2.2 percent and North East 2.2 percent. Two regions alone are home to more than a third of all Ghanaians, which helps explain why jobs, services and investment concentrate where they do.

### 3.6. Life Expectancy

Life expectancy at birth answers a simple question: how many years a newborn would be expected to live if prevailing age-specific mortality rates remained unchanged?

#### Figure 3.9: Survival and longevity over time (Life expectancy rate, 1960 to 2021)

The fastest gain came between 1960 and 1980, when life expectancy rose by 15.7 years as vaccination, clean water, better food and health care spread across the country.

### 4.1. Learning and Opportunity

#### Figure 4.1: School enrolment rates (KG, Primary and JHS)

Source: EMIS, 2019/20 to 2023/24

Gross enrolment counts everyone in a class, whatever their age, against the number of children who should be there. Ghana's difficulty has never been getting children into primary school. ### 4.2. Health and Well-Being

#### Figure 4.4: Health insurance coverage

Source: National Health Insurance Authority(NHIA)

Health insurance coverage measures active membership who could access health services within a defined year. It is distinct from cumulative or ever-registered membership, which counts all persons who have enrolled at any point and is substantially higher. Coverage is expressed as a share of the total population.

Coverage more than doubled over the period. Active membership rose from 30.0% in 2010 to 66.2% in 2025, an increase of 36.2 percentage points.

Growth was not continuous. Coverage climbed to 58.8% by 2015, then declined for three consecutive years to a low of 48.3% in 2018 below the 2012 level. More than ten percentage points were lost without any change to eligibility or benefits.

The scheme then plateaued for six years. Between 2019 and 2024 active membership moved within a narrow band of 52.0% to 55.9%. The 2024 figure (55.9%) remained below the 2015 peak, meaning coverage was lower after nine years than at the start of that period.

2025 marks a break in trend. The single-year gain of 10.3 percentage points is the largest since the early expansion phase and reverses the decade-long plateau.

### 4.3. Living Conditions

#### Figure 4.8: Housing Deficits

Source: GSS, PHC 2010 and 2021

Four ideas are kept separate here.

```text
   i.    Housing stock, or supply, is the number of dwelling units that exist; a dwelling
         unit is the space one household occupies, so a single house can hold several.
  ii.    Housing demand, or the housing requirement, is an estimate of how many
         units the country needs, based on the number of households and
         assumptions about how many people should share a unit.
  iii.   A household is a group of people who live together and share housekeeping.
 iv.     The housing deficit is the gap between the units required and the units
         available.
```

### 5.2. Agriculture

### 5.3. Enterprises in Ghana

5.3.1. 2.64 million Businesses, Three Ways of Working Beyond the farm, much of Ghana’s economic activity takes place in its businesses. Ghana had 2,644,363 businesses in 2024. That is the count from Phase I of the Integrated Business Establishment Survey (IBES I), the first national economic census to count businesses that work outside buildings, not only those inside them.

Think of three kinds of business. An establishment works inside a building or fixed structure: a shop, a workshop, an office. An open-space business works from the same spot every day, but under the open sky: the roadside table of tomatoes and pepper. A mobile business moves while it works: the hawker in traffic, the head porter carrying bread, the trader on a motorbike.

### 5.3.6. What It Means

Let’s break it down. Ghana’s business base is wide and small. There are many businesses, and most are tiny, young and informal. The number of businesses grew faster than the number of jobs inside them. A very small group of large firms engages one in five of the people working in establishments.

### 5.7. Ghana’s Fiscal Operations

How to read these numbers. Think of the government like a household. Money comes in, mostly from taxes. Money goes out to pay workers, settle past loans, and run services such as schools, clinics and roads. When more goes out than comes in, the gap is called a deficit, and the government must borrow to fill it. When the gap shrinks, the government borrows less and owes less over time. This section looks at what came in, what went out, and the gap between them in 2025, set against the years since 2022.

2025 at a glance. 2025 was the year the gap almost closed. On the commitment basis, which counts every bill the government took on during the year, the overall deficit fell from GH¢81.5 billion in 2024 to GH¢13.9 billion in 2025. Against the size of the economy, that is a drop from 6.9 percent of GDP to 1.0 percent, the smallest gap in the four years shown (Tables 5.9a and 5.9b). Money coming in rose by 20.5 percent to GH¢224.9 billion, while money going out fell by 11.0 percent to GH¢238.7 billion. For the first time in these four years, the government took in more than it spent before paying interest on past loans, a primary surplus of 2.5 percent of GDP.

The bigger picture. Tables 5.9 and 5.10 show that the fiscal gap narrowed between 2022 and 2025. On the commitment basis, the overall deficit fell from GH¢72.2 billion, or 11.8 percent of GDP, in 2022 to GH¢13.9 billion, or 1.0 percent of GDP, in 2025. Over the same period, the primary balance moved from a deficit of GH¢26.5 billion, or 4.3 percent of GDP, to a surplus of GH¢36.0 billion, or 2.5 percent of GDP. Revenue and grants remained at 15.7 percent of GDP, while commitment expenditure fell from 27.5 percent to 16.6 percent of GDP. Interest payments declined from 7.4 percent to 3.5 percent of GDP, while capital expenditure fell from 3.0 percent to 1.4 percent. On the cash basis, the 2025 overall deficit was 3.0 percent of GDP and the primary surplus was 0.5 percent.

### 5.7.1. Revenue Performance

More money, but the same slice of the economy. Total money coming in (revenue and grants) rose from GH¢186.6 billion in 2024 to GH¢224.9 billion in 2025, an extra GH¢38.3 billion, or 20.5 percent. But the economy grew almost as fast, from GH¢1,182.8 billion to GH¢1,434.1 billion. So measured against the economy, revenue barely moved, easing from 15.8 percent of GDP to 15.7 percent. Since 2022, revenue has more than doubled in cedis, yet its share of the economy has stayed within a narrow band of 15.3 to 15.8 percent. The government is collecting far more money, but not a bigger slice of a bigger economy.

Taxes are the main source. Tax revenue rose from GH¢151.2 billion in 2024 to GH¢184.0 billion in 2025, up 21.7 percent. Of every 100 cedis the government collected in 2025, about 82 came from taxes. As a share of the economy, tax revenue held steady at 12.8 percent in both 2024 and 2025, up from 12.3 percent in 2022. The IMF notes that the 2026 budget plans to raise a further 0.6 percent of GDP from taxes other than oil.

Other income was mixed and modest. The government also earns money outside taxes. Fees and charges (non-tax revenue) were almost flat at GH¢27.9 billion, slipping from 2.3 to 1.9 percent of GDP. Other revenue more than doubled, from GH¢4.9 billion to GH¢10.3 billion. Grants (gifts from development partners) were just GH¢1.8 billion, less than one cedi in every hundred the government received. This shows that almost all of Ghana’s public money now comes from within the country.

### 5.7.2. Expenditure Performance

Two ways to count spending. Spending can be counted in two ways. The commitment basis counts every bill the government took on during the year, paid or not. The cash basis counts only the money actually paid out, including payments for bills left over from earlier years. In 2025 the two measures moved in opposite directions, and the difference tells the story of the year.

Committed spending fell to its lowest share. On the commitment basis, spending fell by 11.0 percent to GH¢238.7 billion. Against the economy, that is 16.6 percent of GDP, down from 22.7 percent in 2024 and the lowest in the four years. Spending before interest (primary expenditure) fell even faster, by 14.7 percent to GH¢188.8 billion, or 13.2 percent of GDP. The Ministry of Finance said this came from tighter control of spending: fewer ministries and cuts to items it judged non-essential, such as foreign travel, workshops and official vehicles.

Cash spending rose as old bills were cleared. On the cash basis, spending rose by 8.2 percent to GH¢268.3 billion, or 18.7 percent of GDP. The two measures diverged mainly because the government paid off GH¢29.5 billion of old unpaid bills, called arrears, worth 2.1 percent of GDP. This was the reverse of 2024, when unpaid bills built up by GH¢20.1 billion.

Where the money went. The largest cost was paying public workers (salaries, wages and related costs), which rose by 17.5 percent, from GH¢67.2 billion to GH¢79.0 billion, and took about one in every three cedis of committed spending. As a share of the economy it eased slightly, from 5.7 to 5.5 percent. Transfers to other government units rose by 24.3 percent to GH¢57.7 billion (4.0 percent of GDP). Spending on social benefits, which supports people in need, almost quadrupled from GH¢0.7 billion to GH¢2.9 billion, though it stayed small at 0.2 percent of GDP.

Interest took a smaller bite. Interest is the extra cost of past borrowing. In cedis, interest payments rose by 6.6 percent, from GH¢46.8 billion to GH¢49.9 billion. But against the economy they fell from 4.0 to 3.5 percent of GDP, and they claimed a smaller share of revenue, about 22 cedis in every 100 in 2025, down from 25 in 2024. This continues a steep fall since 2022, when interest took 7.4 percent of GDP and about 47 cedis of every 100 of revenue, before Ghana restructured its debt in 2023. The Ministry of Finance reported interest costs of 1.3 percent of GDP by the end of June 2026, below its half-year target of 1.8 percent.

Investment spending was cut back. Capital spending pays for lasting things like roads and buildings. It fell by 31.1 percent, from GH¢29.4 billion to GH¢20.2 billion, or 1.4 percent of GDP, down from 2.5 percent in 2024 and the lowest in the four years. Spending on goods and services was almost halved, from GH¢11.5 billion to GH¢6.1

### 5.7.3. Fiscal Balance

The deficit shrank to its smallest. The overall balance on the commitment basis moved from a deficit of GH¢81.5 billion (6.9 percent of GDP) in 2024 to a deficit of GH¢13.9 billion (1.0 percent) in 2025. The road there was uneven: the deficit narrowed from 11.8 percent of GDP in 2022 to 3.5 percent in 2023, widened again to 6.9 percent in 2024, then fell to 1.0 percent in 2025. On the cash basis it also shrank, from GH¢61.4 billion (5.2 percent) to GH¢43.4 billion (3.0 percent), larger than the commitment figure because part of the year’s money went to clear old bills.

The primary balance turned positive. The primary balance shows whether day-to-day income can cover spending before interest on past loans. On the commitment basis it swung from a deficit of GH¢34.7 billion (2.9 percent of GDP) in 2024 to a surplus of GH¢36.0 billion (2.5 percent) in 2025, a turnaround of GH¢70.7 billion in a single year. On the cash basis it moved from a deficit of GH¢14.6 billion to a surplus of GH¢6.5 billion (0.5 percent of GDP).

Why this matters. A primary surplus means the government can cover its current work from its own income and begin to pay down what it owes. When the IMF completed its sixth and final review in July 2026, it noted that Ghana’s primary balance had swung into surplus and that the risk of debt distress had improved to moderate. The 2026 budget aims to hold a primary surplus of 1.5 percent of GDP on the commitment basis.

What to watch. The 2025 figures also show the trade-offs behind the progress. The gap closed partly because investment was reduced: capital spending fell to its lowest share of the economy in the four years, while workers’ pay and interest together still took more than half of all money coming in. The IMF has observed that the stronger debt path creates room to meet development needs, provided public money is managed well and reforms continue.

### 5.8. Monetary and Financial Sector

In 2025, Ghana’s money and banking system was the calmest it had been in years. Prices rose far more slowly, borrowing became cheaper, lending to businesses and families picked up, banks grew stronger, and the country’s foreign savings climbed to a new high. This built on a recovery that started in 2024, after the hard years of 2022 and 2023 (Table 5.11).

One number ties the whole story together: inflation. Inflation is how fast prices rise; when prices climb quickly, each cedi buys less, so the Bank of Ghana (the central bank) makes borrowing more expensive to cool them down. Inflation had peaked at 54.1% in December 2022, ended 2024 above 20%, then fell to 5.4% by December 2025. As inflation came down, almost everything else followed: money grew more slowly, interest rates dropped, lending revived, and savings rose.

The Bank of Ghana says money grew more slowly in 2025 because it kept borrowing expensive, drew extra cash out of the banks, and the cedi gained value. In the earlier years money had grown very fast for two reasons: the Bank created money to help cover government spending, and the cedi lost value, which made savings held in foreign currencies worth more in cedis.

### 5.8.2. Monetary Policy and Interest Rates

The Bank of Ghana made borrowing much cheaper in 2025, and other lenders followed. The Monetary Policy Rate is the rate the Bank uses to steer the cost of borrowing across the country. It was cut from 27.0% at the end of 2024 to 18.0% by December 2025, a fall of 9 percentage points in a single year. The Bank said it could ease because prices were rising more slowly, people expected the calm to last, and the country’s savings were stronger. The rate had been pushed as high as 30.0% in 2023, when prices were climbing fast.

Other rates fell with it. The rate banks charge each other dropped from 27.0% in 2024 to 16.3% in 2025. The average rate banks charge people and businesses to borrow fell from 30.3% to 20.5%. The gap between that lending rate and the Bank’s own rate narrowed from 3.3 percentage points in 2024 to 2.5 points in 2025, a sign that cheaper policy was reaching real borrowers.

The sharpest drop was in Treasury-bill rates, the interest the government pays to borrow for short periods. The 91-day rate fell from 27.7% in 2024 to 11.1% in 2025, a drop of 16.6 percentage points in one year. The 182-day and 364-day rates also fell, ending 2025 at 12.5% and 12.9%, so loans that take longer to repay still paid a little more. The Bank of Ghana said the lower rates owed partly to efforts to make government borrowing cheaper.

### 5.8.4. Financial-Sector Size and Soundness

Banks were bigger and stronger in 2025 than in 2024, and loans were repaid a little better. The total value of everything the banks own rose from GH¢367.8 billion in 2024 to GH¢446.9 billion in 2025, a gain of 21.5% in one year.

### 7.3. Average Monthly Rainfall

Monthly rainfall refers to the amount of rain received within a month, measured in millimetres (mm). Rainfall is important for agricultural planning, as farmers use information on the timing and amount of rainfall to guide planting and other farm activities, while water managers use it to prepare for potential floods, droughts and changes in water availability.

In 2025, rainfall increased gradually from 11.1 mm in January to a first peak of 216.7 mm in June, before declining sharply to 67.2 mm in July and 71.9 mm in August. Rainfall then increased again, reaching a second peak of 185.1 mm in October, before declining to 66.6 mm in November and 57.3 mm in December.

This pattern reflects the seasonal variation in rainfall across Ghana, particularly the two rainy seasons experienced in much of southern Ghana, while northern Ghana generally experiences one main rainy season. The two pronounced rainfall peaks in June and October have important implications for agricultural activities, water-resource management and the timing of planting and harvesting.

### 9. Thematic Deep Dive: The Youth Bulge

▪ Who Is a Youth? ▪ Implication for the labour market ▪ Youth in agriculture

<a id="pdf-page-129"></a>

<!-- pdf-page: 129; printed-page: 108 -->

## PDF page 129 — printed page 108

A Window of Opportunity or a Growing Pressure For twenty-five years Ghana's population has been changing shape. Picture the country as a staircase with one step for every age group. Twenty-five years ago, the bottom steps, the children, were the widest. Those children have grown up, and the widest steps have moved up into the youth and young adult ages. That single change carries both a promise and a warning.

Fewer Children, More Youth | Youth Bulge Hypothesis In 2000, children aged 0 to 14 made up 41.8 percent of the population. By 2025 they are 35.0 percent. Nothing dramatic happened; families simply began having fewer children. The result is what demographers call a youth bulge, a population crowded into the young ages, whether those are counted as 15 to 24 or as 15 to 35.

Who Is a Youth?

Ages 15–24 (International Definition) By the international definition of 15 to 24 years, youth stood at about 20 percent of the population in 2025 (some 6.6 million people) and has held around that level across the periods shown in Figure 3.2, at about 20 percent in both 2010 and 2021. That keeps Ghana inside the 15 to 20 percent band used to define a youth bulge.

Ages 15–35 (African Union Definition) By the African Union definition of 15 to 35 years, more than one Ghanaian in every three has been a young person for over two decades. The share has stayed around 35 percent across the periods shown in Figure 3.2, at about 35 percent in 2010, 36 percent in 2021 and 35 percent in 2025 (about 12 million).

Two futures emerge: Demographic Dividend (Opportunity)

The first future is the dividend. If Ghana expands good schooling, builds technical and vocational skills, creates enough jobs and backs young entrepreneurs, a large young population becomes an engine of growth. With fewer children to support, families and government can spend more on each child and each worker. That is a demographic dividend: faster growth produced by the shape of the population itself.

Demographic Pressure (Risk) The second future is the reverse. If jobs do not grow as fast as the number of young people, pressure builds on the jobs that already exist. Between 2005 and 2015 Ghana had exactly those conditions: a high share of youth, fewer children to support, and more people arriving at the labour market every year.

<a id="pdf-page-130"></a>

## PDF page 130 — printed page 109

When an economy cannot absorb them, the consequences follow a familiar order: high youth unemployment and underemployment, informal and risky work, more migration within the country and beyond it, rising frustration, and in time political instability. The problem is never that young people exist. It is that opportunity did not grow alongside them.

Ghana’s Youth Bulge The youth (ages 15 to 35) share stood at about 35 percent of the population in 2025, roughly 12 million young people out of 33.7 million, and has held broadly steady across the periods shown in Figure 3.2, at about 35 percent in 2010 and 36 percent in 2021, even as the absolute number of young people has continued to grow. Ghana is not a mature population; it is early in the transition after the bulge, still youth-heavy but slowly ageing. Because the child share has fallen while the youth share has held steady, the share of children aged 0 to 14 fell from 41.8 percent in 2000 to 35.0 percent in 2025, there are now roughly as many young people aged 15 to 35 as children aged 0 to 14, a ratio of about one to one, up from about 0.9 in 2010. In plain terms, even as families have had fewer children, the demand for jobs and training has stayed high for a quarter of a century.

The Core Challenge: Employment and Productivity The youth bulge is, at bottom, a jobs question. Each year a large group joins the labour force, and the economy must create enough productive work to take them in. Growth by itself is not enough. If it comes from sectors that use machines rather than people, the dividend is lost. Turning a population advantage into an economic one takes labour-intensive industry, modern agriculture, far more technical and vocational training, closer links between schools and employers, real support for young entrepreneurs, and labour institutions that function.

With investment →Demographic dividend. Without investment → Demographic pressure This moment will not last. In the decades ahead, today's young people will move into the older working ages. If they are skilled, employed and productive, they will carry the economy. If they are not, Ghana will carry the cost of their lost years for a generation. A youth bulge is neither a blessing nor a crisis in itself; it is a conditional opportunity. Ghana has had one of the longest sustained youth bulges in its history, and what the country earns from it depends entirely on how well it invests in young people now. The population has created the opening. Policy will decide the outcome.

Implications for the Labour Market Ghana's population is heavily concentrated in the younger ages. In Ghana, youth mean anyone aged 15 to 35, in line with the National Youth Policy and the African Union definition. This group is the country's main store of human capital and its most likely source of economic transformation. Its growth brings real opportunity and real strain at the same time.

The analysis below draws on the 2022 Annual Household Income and Expenditure Survey and the 2025 Quarterly Labour Force Survey. It looks at youth unemployment, young people not in employment, education or training, labour underutilisation, informal work, and how well education and skills match the jobs available.

Ghana's youth population keeps growing, so the supply of labour grows every year. Large groups leave school and compete for the same jobs. In 2025, unemployment among 15-to 24-year-olds was above 30 percent, far higher than the national average, and above 20 percent for the wider 15 to 35 group. First-time jobseekers face the hardest wall: no work experience, weak bridges from school to work, and too few openings in the formal economy.

Youth Unemployment Rate by Age Group, 2025 Unemployment is only part of the picture. NEET refers to young people who are neither employed nor enrolled in education or training. In the third quarter of 2025, close to two million Ghanaians aged 15 to 35 were in this group, representing 19.5 percent of that age group. This quarterly rate is different from the 17.9 percent average recorded across the first three quarters of 2025.

The kind of work available matters as much as the amount. More than 70 percent of all employment in Ghana is informal, and young people are over-represented in it. Informal work usually means no written contract, no job security, irregular pay and no pension. It absorbs labour that the formal economy cannot, which is useful, but it also holds back productivity, raises little tax revenue and teaches few transferable skills.

The headline unemployment rate understates the problem. Many young people in informal or part-time work want more hours and cannot get them. Add that underemployment to open unemployment and to those available but not searching, and a large share of Ghana's young labour capacity is not being fully used.

Skills are the other half of the story. About 65 percent of employed youth say their skills fit the job, while 8 percent are overqualified and 15 percent underqualified. Only 58 percent say their level of education matches what the job demands. Ghana is not short of educated young people; it is short of education matched to the work that exists. That is an argument for stronger technical and vocational training built around real employer demand.

A rapidly growing supply of young workers can increase competition for available jobs, particularly where job creation does not keep pace with the number of people entering the labour market. This can contribute to unemployment, underemployment and pressure on wages, especially in lower-skilled occupations. Prolonged unemployment can also weaken skills and work experience, making it harder for young people to find suitable employment and potentially reducing their future earnings.

Gender and geography deepen the divide. Young women are more likely than young men to be inactive or NEET, even as their schooling improves. Greater Accra and Ashanti record higher youth unemployment and NEET rates because that is where young jobseekers gather. Any effective response must therefore be shaped by gender and targeted by place.

Taken together, the evidence shows that Ghana has not yet fully realised a demographic dividend. Youth unemployment remains high, and close to two million people aged 15 to 35 were neither employed nor enrolled in education or training in the third quarter of 2025. Informal work accounts for more than 70 percent of employment, and many workers report a mismatch between their education and their jobs. These indicators point to continuing difficulty in creating enough suitable work for young people.

Ghana's youth bulge is therefore both an opportunity and a challenge. The demographic conditions for economic gain are in place: a growing workforce, rising consumption, energy for enterprise and innovation. Collecting that gain requires steady, coordinated action to create productive jobs, align skills with work, formalise informal employment and close gender and regional gaps.

Taken together, the evidence points one way. Without deliberate action, high youth unemployment, informal work and mismatched skills may continue to weigh on productivity. Investment in labour institutions, technical and vocational education, enterprise support and inclusive economic policy would help the economy make fuller use of a large young population.

The number of 15-to 24-year-olds working in agriculture, forestry and fishing dropped to 381,476, less than half the 2010 figure. The 15 to 35 group fell to 1,222,885. In eleven years, Ghana's young people moved away from farming.

The gender gap widened as they left. Among 15-to 24-year-olds in 2021 there were 243,364 males and 138,112 females, a sharper male dominance than in 2010. In the 15 to 35 group there were 764,212 males and 458,673 females. Young women left the sector faster than young men.

Several factors may be associated with this shift: better-paying options elsewhere, migration to the towns, and perceptions of farming as offering limited prospects. These are possible explanations rather than measured causes. Two trends stand out. Fewer young people farm, and those who do are increasingly male.

This matters well beyond agriculture. Ghana cannot feed a population heading for 33.7 million with an ageing farm workforce. Making farming attractive to young people, and to young women in particular, means machines instead of hoes, agribusiness rather than subsistence, credit that actually reaches a 22-year-old, and a place in modern value chains. Food security, rural jobs and the future of the sector all rest on it.

### 10.1. Clean Water Was 59 Minutes Away

Now It's a Tap : The Story of Mangoase School in the Ada West District, Greater Accra Region, From Data to Decisions Every morning, before a single lesson began at Mangoase School, time was already being lost. Teachers and children made the walk, fifteen minutes each way, to collect water from the community stream. A stream shared with animals, untreated and unsafe. On better days, someone made the longer thirty-minute trip to a nearby community for cleaner water, though "cleaner" was never a guarantee by the time the journey home was done. Either way, learning waited and every day it waited, a school already struggling fell a little further behind.

This was no secret, but without data it was only a story. In October 2023 the Ghana Statistical Service published its Water and Sanitation Report. Inside was a number that could not be ignored: residents of Ada West district spent an average of 59 minutes a day just getting water, among the worst-served districts in the country. Stand-In-the-Gap, a Christian NGO looking for a community to serve in 2024, read that report. They did not guess or follow instinct. They followed the data to Ada West, and then to Mangoase. A scouting visit in January 2024 confirmed what the numbers had already said: a school without clean water, children learning under a shed with no walls, a littered compound, a community worn down by the daily work of getting by. The response was practical. A borehole was drilled. Storage tanks and pipes carried clean underground water straight to the school. A proper classroom replaced the roofless shed. Waste bins brought order to the compound.

Today the children of Mangoase drink clean water without leaving the school grounds. They sit in a real classroom and learn without interruption. Not one of them knows the name of the report that started it. Somewhere between a government dataset and a drilled borehole, official statistics did exactly what they exist to do. They found the people who needed them most, and something changed.

This is what data, in the right hands, can do

### 10.2. Inspiring a New Generation of Data Users

The StatsBank Hackathon was set up by the Ghana Statistical Service and Statistics Denmark to answer one question: what will students do with official statistics if you hand them over? ### 11.1. Introduction

Every chapter before this one answers the question: what is happening? This chapter answers the harder question that comes next: so what, and who should do something about it? It adds no new data. It takes the evidence already shown in this volume and sets out the ten things that stand out when the figures are read together rather than one chapter at a time.

What This Chapter Is, and What It Is Not The Ghana Statistical Service produces the evidence; it does not choose the policy. This chapter says what the statistics show, where the pressure points and the opportunities are, and which broad direction the evidence points to. It stops short, on purpose, of choosing between one policy tool and another.

The Service is saying: this is what the numbers are, this is the way they are moving, this is who is being left behind, and this is what would have to change for the trend to change.

The Service is not saying: which tax, subsidy, rule, programme or budget line should be used to do any of it. Whether a goal is best reached through public spending, rules, incentives or working with the private sector is a decision for Cabinet, Parliament, ministries and agencies, and the district assemblies, who answer to citizens for those choices. The evidence narrows the question. It does not settle it.

### 11.2. Policy Implications

Each policy implication below uses the same four parts: what the numbers say, why it matters, what the evidence suggests for assessment, and how progress will be tracked. The last part matters most.

An implication that cannot be measured cannot be watched, so every action below is tied to a number the National Statistical System already produces, with its most recent value given as the starting point against which future editions of Ghana in Numbers will report.

What the numbers say. More than 11 million Ghanaians, about one in three people, are aged 15 to 35. Over the same three quarters, the average NEET rate for people aged 15 to 35 was 17.9 percent. In the third quarter alone, close to two million people aged 15 to 35 were neither employed nor enrolled in education or training, and women made up more than 60 percent of this group. Why it matters. The fall in the child share is what creates a chance called a demographic dividend: fewer dependants for every worker, so more can be invested in each one. ### 2. Agriculture Feeds the Country but Is Not Financed Like It

What the numbers say. Food insecurity rose from 37.7 percent in 2024 to 39.8 percent in 2025, affecting about 13.0 million people.

Why it matters. A sector that provides a fifth of national output, most of the food supply and a large share of rural livelihoods, yet receives so little public spending, raises the question of whether public money matches farming’s stated goals. Smaller households add to the pressure: a falling household size raises the number of homes needed even when population growth slows.

### 4. Human Development Gains Are Real, and They Are Reversible

What the numbers say. The direction in health and education is strongly upward. Mothers dying in childbirth fell from 485 per 100,000 live births in 2010 to 301 in 2021, deaths of children under five halved from 80 to 40 per 1,000, and newborn deaths fell from 43 per 1,000 in 2003 to 17 in 2022. Active health insurance membership rose from 30.0 percent in 2010 to 66.2 percent in 2025. Junior high completion rose from 77.5 percent to 88.4 percent in five years. But the trends are not all one way. Insurance coverage reached 58.8 percent in 2015, then fell for three years in a row to 48.3 percent in 2018 before recovering. Junior high net enrolment fell to a record low of 44.3 percent in 2021/22. Both primary and junior high completion dipped after 2022/23. And food insecurity rose again in 2025 after falling between 2022 and 2024.

Why it matters. Every one of these reversals happened without any change in policy aim. Coverage is not won once; it is re-won every year through re-enrolment, keeping people in the system, and steady service. The mid-decade drop in insurance is the clearest warning in this volume: more than ten percentage points were lost between 2015 and 2018 with no change to who was eligible or what was covered, showing that gains can be given back if the machinery behind them slips.

What the evidence suggests for assessment. The evidence suggests policymakers should look at treating the holding of coverage as a priority in its own right, not just a side effect of expansion, and at building routine early warning of reversals into how programmes are watched, so a dip is spotted in the year it happens rather than in the next survey. Which agency owns that job, and how it is funded, is an institutional decision.

How progress will be tracked. Active health insurance membership (starting point 66.2 percent, 2025); maternal mortality ratio (301 per 100,000 live births, 2021); under-five mortality rate (40 per 1,000, 2022); completion rates at primary, junior high and senior high; food insecurity (39.8 percent, 2025).

### 9. Monetary Conditions: Inflation and Interest Rates Fell, While Credit Remained Low Relative to GDP

What the numbers say. Inflation fell from 54.1 percent in December 2022 to 5.4 percent in December 2025. The Bank of Ghana lowered its main interest rate from 27.0 percent to 18.0 percent during 2025, the 91-day Treasury-bill rate fell from 27.7 percent to 11.1 percent, and the average lending rate fell from 30.3 percent to 20.5 percent. After removing the effect of rising prices, lending to businesses and families grew by 13.1 percent in 2025, following falls of 14.5 percent in 2022 and 10.2 percent in 2023. Under the Bank of Ghana’s gross international reserves measure, reserves rose from US$5.9 billion in 2023 to US$13.8 billion in 2025, providing 5.7 months of import cover. Reserve figures published by other institutions may differ because their definitions and coverage are not always the same. The banking sector’s capital adequacy ratio recovered to 17.5 percent. Even so, lending to businesses and families was 7.4 percent of GDP in 2025, down from 10.4 percent in 2022, and 18.9 percent of bank loans were not being repaid, compared with 16.0 percent in 2022.

Why it matters. Lower inflation and interest rates make borrowing cheaper for families, firms and government, and larger savings help the economy cope with shocks from abroad. Lending is one of the main ways that stability reaches real investment in the farms, firms, homes and jobs discussed earlier. A low level of lending compared with the economy, and a high share of loans not being repaid, limit how much that channel is used. The 2022–2023 period also shows how quickly these numbers can turn.

What the evidence suggests for assessment. The evidence suggests policymakers and regulators should look at how to keep inflation low and savings strong while lending more to businesses and families, and how to reduce loans that are not repaid so that bank capital can support new lending. The choice of monetary, banking and budget tools is a matter for the Bank of Ghana, the Ministry of Finance and Parliament.

How progress will be tracked. Inflation (starting point 5.4 percent, December 2025); main interest rate (18.0 percent); average lending rate (20.5 percent); lending to businesses and families after inflation (growth of 13.1 percent); that lending as a share of GDP (7.4 percent); share of bank loans not being repaid (18.9 percent); banks’ safety-money ratio (17.5 percent); foreign savings (US$13.8 billion, 5.7 months of import cover).

The evidence suggests policymakers should look at what helps micro businesses grow into small and medium ones, at making registration simpler and more worthwhile, especially for businesses started since 2015, at extending digital finance beyond Greater Accra, and at the circumstances of children working as mobile traders. Which mix of registration, tax, credit and support measures to use is a decision for Government, the regulators and the district assemblies.

Five Decisions Ghana's Numbers Should Influence Statistics earn their cost when they change a decision. Five decisions that come up again and again in Ghanaian public life should increasingly be led by evidence, and this volume already holds what is needed to lead them.

### 1. Where government spends. Poverty, population, infrastructure and service

numbers should guide which places and which groups are targeted. The poverty map now covers all 261 districts, so money can follow measured need rather than past habits or national averages. The fiscal figures show why targeting matters: in 2025, salaries and interest together took 57 of every 100 cedis of revenue and grants,


---

<a id="pdf-page-156"></a>

<!-- pdf-page: 156; printed-page: 135 -->

## PDF page 156 — printed page 135

and capital spending fell to 1.4 percent of GDP, so the money left for other priorities is limited.

### 2. Where Ghana invests. Production, business, trade, labour and productivity data

should show which sectors can create jobs and raise output per worker. The evidence is pointed: exports rest on three goods worth almost four cedis in every five, raw cocoa beans are 8.6 percent of exports while processed cocoa paste is only 2.3 percent, and crude oil is sold only to buy refined fuel back. The business census adds a second signal: nine in ten establishments are micro businesses, while only 4,874 large firms engage about one in five people working in establishments. Trade with Ghana’s neighbours adds a third: a first survey of informal cross-border trade found that goods worth GH¢38.4 billion crossed the land borders with Togo, Burkina Faso and Côte d’Ivoire in the year to September 2025, more than official trade with those three countries, with drinks and fuel going out and food, led by cooking oil, coming in. The evidence suggests policymakers should look at how making more at home, and helping small businesses grow, could add jobs and earnings.

### 3. Who receives support. Broken-down evidence, not national averages alone,

should show who may need support. A national teenage motherhood rate of 12.8 percent includes a rural rate of 17.2 percent. In the third quarter of 2025, women made up more than 60 percent of people aged 15 to 35 who were neither employed nor enrolled in education or training. District data also showed that 23 percent of multidimensional poverty in Afigya Kwabre North was linked to a lack of health insurance.

### 4. Where services are expanded. Population and demographic numbers should

decide where schools, health centres, homes, transport and other infrastructure go. Ghana adds between half a million and 700,000 people a year, is 58.7 percent urban, and has an average household size of 3.6 and falling. Each of these figures change where, and how much, must be built.

### 5. How success is measured. Big policies and programmes should start with a

measurable starting point and named indicators, and be checked against them at set times. Every indicator in this chapter is offered as such a starting point. A programme that cannot say what it will change, from what value, by when, cannot be shown to have worked. Farming is the clearest single test: it supplies 22.8 percent of GDP and rising output, yet received about 0.2 percent of GDP in public spending in 2024. Public finances now have their own starting points too, including the 2.5 percent of GDP primary surplus and the 1.4 percent of GDP capital spending recorded in 2025.

What We Still Need to Know An honest account of the evidence includes an account of its limits. The gaps below are visible in this volume, and closing them is part of the work the numbers call for.

Earnings and productivity. The labour statistics show whether people work, not what they earn. Ghana has no regular series on wages, on how income is spread among those in work, or on output per worker by sector, which makes it impossible to say whether rising participation is turning into rising living standards.

Learning outcomes. Enrolment, completion and pupil-to-trained-teacher ratios measure access to school. None of them measures what pupils actually learn. A completion rate of 88.4 percent at junior high says nothing about the skills of those who complete.

The economy below the national level. Poverty is now measured for all 261 districts, and IBES I now counts businesses by region, but output and jobs are not measured at district level. There is no district-level GDP or employment series to set beside the district poverty map.

Internal migration. Urban growth, the move of young people to towns and the departure of youth from farming are all put down to migration in this volume, but Ghana has no regular internal-migration series with which to measure or forecast those flows.

The informal economy in detail. Informal work is more than 70 percent of all jobs, and IBES I has now counted about 777,000 open-space and mobile businesses, yet the finances of those businesses, informal earnings, output per worker, and how often workers move from informal to formal jobs are largely unmeasured. Phase II of the survey is expected to add more detail on business finances. Cross-border trade has started to come into view: since October 2024 the Service has measured informal trade at 321 border points, but counting is limited to daytime crossings and leaves out night trade and hawked food, so the true value is likely higher. The single largest part of Ghana’s labour market is the least well described.

Housing supply. The housing shortage is worked out from estimated demand. There is no regular measure of homes completed, of building in informal settlements, or of rents, so the supply side of the shortage is inferred rather than observed.

Disability and other under-counted groups. The DACF Support Survey reached 7,361 people across 10 districts in 3 ecological zones. It is the most complete citizen-generated dataset of its kind in Ghana, and it is not nationally representative. People with disabilities, migrants and other under-counted groups still lack full national coverage.

Currency and frequency of environmental data. Regional forest-cover shares and carbon figures run to 2021 and the coastal indicator to 2020. Ghana has no regular series on greenhouse-gas emissions or on waste and plastics, which are exactly the measures a climate commitment is reported against.

Timeliness across the board. Several numbers in this volume are the most recent available but are already some years old: tourism to 2023, child marriage and gender-based violence measured only every few years, and youth participation in farming drawn from the 2021 Census. Shortening the time between collecting data and publishing it is itself one of the actions this chapter calls for.

A National Call to Action The numbers in this volume show population and economic growth, longer life expectancy and changes in public participation. They also show that many young people are not in productive work. The report presents these findings together because they are part of the same national picture.

None of it changes anything on its own. A statistic on stunting becomes a health budget only when someone reads it and acts. A district poverty figure becomes a borehole only when a district assembly uses it. The figures in these pages are not a record of the past. They are a tool for deciding what happens next, and they will be judged by the decisions they improve.

Know the numbers. Use the numbers. Change the outcomes.
